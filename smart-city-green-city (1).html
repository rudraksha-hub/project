Smart City, Green City?

Can technology really make our cities sustainable?

India's Smart Cities Mission promised cleaner, more efficient urban life through sensors, data and digital infrastructure. A decade in, we looked at what the technology has actually delivered — and what it still can't fix.

where cities are struggling

The problem with the modern Indian city

Rapid urbanisation has outpaced planning. Four pressures show up in almost every growing city.

Peak-hour traffic in Bengaluru — a scene familiar to almost every growing Indian city

Traffic & emissions

Growing vehicle numbers mean longer commutes, constant congestion and rising air pollution from exhaust fumes.

Water wastage

Old, leaking pipelines and untracked distribution lose huge volumes of treated water before it reaches homes.

Waste management

Inconsistent collection schedules and poor route planning leave garbage piling up in many neighbourhoods.

Loss of green spaces

Parks and tree cover are steadily replaced by concrete as cities expand outward, reducing natural cooling.

the idea behind the mission

What is a "smart city," really?

A smart city uses digital technology and data — sensors, cameras, automated systems — to run services like water, transport, energy and waste more efficiently, so that urban life becomes cleaner, safer and easier to manage.

India launched the Smart Cities Mission in 2015 under the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs, aiming to build core infrastructure and give citizens a decent quality of life through smart solutions.

tech in the pipeline, literally

Smart water

Sensors placed along water pipelines feed live data into a SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) system — a control room that tracks pressure, flow and quality across the network in real time, flagging leaks before they waste large volumes of water.

17,000+
km of water supply networks now monitored through SCADA

Figure as reported across 31 smart cities.

tracking the truck, not just the trash

Smart waste

Garbage trucks fitted with RFID tags and GPS let city control rooms see exactly which routes have been covered, in real time — replacing paper logs and guesswork with verified, trackable collection.

9,194+
RFID-enabled vehicles now used for automatic vehicle location
  • Missed streets are flagged instantly instead of going unnoticed
  • Route data helps cities plan more efficient collection schedules

powering the city more wisely

Smart energy

Smart cities are shifting toward power that costs less to run and less to the planet.

Rooftop solar panels on homes in Mehsana, Gujarat — small installations add up across a city

Renewable energy

Rooftop solar panels on public buildings and municipal land feed clean power into city operations.

Energy efficiency

Older municipal buildings and systems are retrofitted with efficient equipment that draws less power.

Smart street lighting

LED streetlights with automated controls dim or brighten based on need, cutting wasted electricity at night.

Lower running costs

Reduced power bills free up municipal budgets for other citizen services and maintenance.

getting around, better

Smart mobility

Moving people efficiently — not just moving vehicles — is the real goal of smart transport.

A dedicated, separated cycle track — the same idea behind India's new cycling infrastructure
  • Public transport upgrades, including better bus networks and real-time tracking for commuters.
  • Intelligent Traffic Management Systems (ITMS) that monitor signals and congestion to keep vehicles moving.
  • Cycling infrastructure that gives people a safer, low-emission way to travel short distances.

the part no sensor can replace

Green spaces

No amount of software substitutes for trees, parks and open land. They do work that infrastructure alone cannot.

A tree-lined path in Green Park, New Delhi — no sensor can replace what a canopy like this does

Cool the air

Tree cover lowers local temperatures and softens the urban heat island effect.

Support wellbeing

Parks and walking areas give people space to rest, exercise and socialise.

Absorb rainwater

Soil and roots soak up rain, reducing waterlogging and easing pressure on drains.

Clean the air

Vegetation filters pollutants and adds oxygen back into dense urban air.

a necessary pause

Reality check

Technology helps cities run better — but it is not, by itself, the same thing as sustainability.

Smart ≠ automatically sustainable

Every sensor, server and control room comes with its own cost, upkeep and footprint. Sustainability depends on how — and for whom — that technology is used.

₹ Cost

Sensors, control rooms and digital systems are expensive to install across an entire city.

⚙ Maintenance

Hardware needs constant upkeep and trained staff, or systems quietly stop working.

⚡ Electricity demand

Cameras, sensors and servers all run on power — some of it undoing the energy savings elsewhere.

📶 Digital access

Not every resident can use app-based services, risking a divide between connected and unconnected citizens.

🌳 Natural spaces

Smart infrastructure can still be built at the cost of trees and open land if planning isn't careful.

🤝 People, not just tech

Systems only work if citizens actually use and maintain them responsibly.

why this matters globally

Connecting to the Sustainable Development Goals

The Smart Cities Mission sits directly at the intersection of two UN Sustainable Development Goals.

9

Industry, Innovation & Infrastructure

Smart cities build resilient infrastructure and apply innovation — SCADA systems, RFID tracking, ITMS — to modernise how essential services are delivered.

11

Sustainable Cities & Communities

The end goal is a city that is inclusive, safe and environmentally sound — with clean water, managed waste, green space and mobility for everyone.

SDG 9 provides the tools and infrastructure; SDG 11 is the outcome those tools are meant to serve. One without the other is incomplete — infrastructure without a sustainability goal is just automation, and a sustainability goal without infrastructure is just intention.
